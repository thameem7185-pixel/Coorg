Coorg

by Lokesh Abrol

Chapter 5 — Glimpses of India, Part II

"Coorg" is the second of three travel sketches that make up Glimpses of India, alongside "A Baker from Goa" and "Tea from Assam." Written by Lokesh Abrol, it reads less like a textbook lesson and more like a traveller's notebook — carrying the reader through the district's misty hills, its coffee-scented air, its rivers, and the distinct customs of the people who call it home.

Across the chapter, the author paints Coorg as a place where geography, history and everyday life are impossible to separate.

02

Where earth meets the mist

Location and geography of Coorg

Coorg — known today by its older name, Kodagu — is India's smallest district, folded into the Western Ghats of Karnataka. Its rolling hills, thick forest cover and cool, damp air set it apart from the plains around it, giving the district a climate closer to a hill station than to tropical India.

The terrain rises and falls in ridges cloaked in coffee estates, rainforest and spice gardens, making Coorg feel remote and self-contained even though it sits well within reach of the rest of Karnataka.

03

A cradle of monsoons

Climate and evergreen rainforests

Coorg is often called the cradle of the monsoons, since it catches rain from both the south-west and the retreating north-east monsoon, leaving the district green and well-watered for much of the year. This steady rainfall sustains dense evergreen rainforest across its slopes.

Winter, from September to March, is considered the most pleasant season to visit — cool, clear and comfortable — while the rains bring the forest fully alive, swelling streams and waterfalls across the hills.

04

Dress that carries history

Traditional cultural attire and the kupya

Kodava men traditionally wear the kupya, a long, dark, robe-like coat cinched at the waist with a striking red-and-gold sash called the chele kathi. Tucked into that sash sits a curved dagger, the peechekathi — a mark of the community's martial tradition worn even on ceremonial occasions.

Women, too, have a distinctive way of draping the sari, with the pleats folded at the back rather than the front and the loose end pinned over the shoulder — a style found nowhere else in the country and still worn with pride at Kodava weddings and festivals.

05

A puzzle historians still argue over

Greek and Arab descent theories

The ancestry of the Kodavas has never been settled with certainty, and two competing theories are usually offered. One holds that they descend from a fragment of Alexander the Great's army, left behind after his campaigns and gradually settled into the hills — a theory some connect to the flowing, robe-like cut of traditional Kodava dress.

The other points to Arab roots, drawing on the community's long-standing martial customs and its comfort with carrying arms. Neither theory has ever been proven outright, and the chapter treats the mystery itself as part of what makes the Kodavas distinct.

06

The aroma of the hills

Coffee plantations and spices

Coffee is the crop most associated with Coorg, grown in the shade of taller silver oak and orange trees across the district's slopes. Pepper vines climb the same trees, and cardamom fills the valleys in between — a landscape built almost entirely on fragrance.

Cherries are hand-picked once ripe, usually between November and February, then processed and dried before roasting — a slow, largely manual chain of work that still shapes the rhythm of Coorg's plantation communities each spring, when the coffee blossom briefly covers the estates in white.

07

The people of the hills

Military valour and the arms tradition

The Kodavas are widely regarded as one of India's martial communities, with their own dress, language — Kodava Takk — and customs found nowhere else in the country. Coorg is often described as the one part of India where households have traditionally been allowed to keep firearms without a license.

Few districts of its size have sent so many of their sons into the highest ranks of the armed forces, among them Field Marshal K. M. Cariappa and General K. S. Thimayya, both of whom led the Indian Army. The community keeps its own festival calendar — Kaveri Sankramana for the river's birth, and Kailpodh, a day set aside to clean and honour the weapons every Kodava household keeps.

A river's birth, a forest's cover

River Kaveri and local wildlife

At Talakaveri, on the slopes of the Brahmagiri range, a modest spring marks the birthplace of the river Kaveri, which winds on across southern India toward the Bay of Bengal. Along its banks near Coorg, the Dubare Elephant Camp is where forest elephants are trained and bathed in the shallows.

Coorg sits within the Western Ghats, one of the world's recognised biodiversity hotspots. In its protected forests — the Nagarhole National Park and the Brahmagiri Wildlife Sanctuary chief among them — tigers, spotted deer, elephants and gaur move through cover so dense that a sighting still feels like an event.

A hilltop view, an unlikely neighbour

Brahmagiri views and the Bylakuppe settlement

A trek up the Brahmagiri hills rewards climbers with a sweep of forest and, on a clear day, a distant view stretching all the way to the Arabian Sea near Kannur. The same hills feed the Kaveri, which downstream offers rafting, canoeing and rock climbing for visitors chasing a little adventure.

Not far away lies Bylakuppe, home to one of India's largest Tibetan refugee settlements and the golden-roofed Namdroling Monastery — an unexpected pocket of Tibetan culture thriving quietly within Coorg's hills.
