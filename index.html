Chapter 5 — Glimpses of India, Part II
01

Where earth meets the mist

A hill country in the Western Ghats

Coorg — known today by its older name, Kodagu — lies folded into the Western Ghats of Karnataka. Its rolling hills, evergreen forest cover and cool, damp air set it apart from the plains around it, giving the district a climate closer to a hill station than to tropical India.

At Talakaveri, on the slopes of the Brahmagiri range, a modest spring marks the birthplace of the river Cauvery, which then winds its way across southern India toward the Bay of Bengal — a quiet beginning for a river central to the life of the region.

02

A land shaped by history

Ruled by its own rajas, tested by empires

For generations before independence, Coorg was governed by its own line of chieftains, the Haleri Rajas, rather than by any distant court. Its steep terrain and thick forest cover made the kingdom hard to reach and harder still to conquer, and it held its ground against Tipu Sultan's advancing forces in the late eighteenth century.

Under British administration in the nineteenth century, coffee was planted across its hillsides for the first time, setting the district on a new economic path that still shapes it today. Coorg entered independent India as a separate state before being merged into Karnataka in 1956.

Places to wander, trails to chase

Tourist trails and the adventure between them

Coorg rewards slow travel. Its landmarks are spread across misty ridgelines and river valleys, so a visit here is as much about the road between places as the places themselves.

Talakaveri

The spring source of the river Cauvery, high on the Brahmagiri hills.

Abbey Falls

A waterfall dropping through coffee and cardamom plantations near Madikeri.

Raja's Seat

A garden viewpoint once favoured by Coorg's kings to watch the sunset.

Namdroling Golden Temple

A Tibetan Buddhist monastery at Bylakuppe, built by a resettled community.

Dubare Elephant Camp

A riverside camp on the Kaveri where forest elephants are trained and bathed.

A jeep-track viewpoint and Coorg's highest peak, both popular treks.

03

The people of the hills

A warrior spirit, quietly carried

The Kodavas, the original people of this land, are widely regarded as one of India's martial communities, with their own dress, language — Kodava Takk — and customs found nowhere else in the country. Coorg is often described as the one part of India where households have traditionally been allowed to keep firearms without a license.

Few districts of its size have sent so many of their sons into the highest ranks of the armed forces, among them Field Marshal K. M. Cariappa and General K. S. Thimayya, both of whom led the Indian Army. That same community keeps its own festival calendar — Kaveri Sankramana for the river's birth, and Kailpodh, a day set aside to clean and honour the weapons every Kodava household keeps.

An emerald wilderness

Forests that shelter more than they show

Coorg lies within the Western Ghats, one of the world's recognised biodiversity hotspots. Within its protected forests — the Nagarhole National Park and the Brahmagiri Wildlife Sanctuary chief among them — tigers, spotted deer, elephants and gaur move through cover so dense that a sighting still feels like an event.

Early-morning safaris along the park's quiet roads are the best chance of seeing this life up close: deer grazing in forest clearings, elephant herds crossing at dawn, and, on rarer mornings, a tiger walking the very track your jeep is on.

04

The aroma of the hills

Coffee, pepper and the scent of spring

Introduced during British rule, coffee has become the crop most associated with Coorg, grown in the shade of silver oak and orange trees across the district's slopes. Pepper vines climb the same trees, and cardamom plantations fill the valleys in between — a landscape built almost entirely on fragrance.

Each spring, coffee blossoms briefly cover the estates in white, and for a few days the entire district smells of it. Coorg remains one of India's principal coffee-growing regions, and the plantations are as much a part of its identity as its rivers or its hills.

How the harvest becomes the coffee

From flowering estate to processed bean

Coffee in Coorg is grown under a canopy of taller shade trees, which keep the plants cool and protect the soil — a traditional method that also lets pepper vines and cardamom share the same land. Flowering happens after the first pre-monsoon showers, usually in March, when entire hillsides turn white for a few days.

Cherries are hand-picked once ripe, typically between November and February, then either dry-processed in the sun or wet-processed by pulping and fermenting before drying. The dried beans are hulled, graded and sent on for roasting — a slow, largely manual chain of work that still shapes the rhythm of the district's plantation communities.

05

In closing

The soul of Coorg

What stays with a visitor to Coorg is rarely one single sight. It is the mist settling over the hills at dawn, a river beginning quietly at a mountain spring, a community that has kept its own customs intact while sending more than its share of soldiers and leaders into the story of modern India.

Geography, history, faith and harvest all meet in this one small district — and it is that quiet layering, more than any single landmark, that the writers of "Glimpses of India" seem to have wanted their readers to notice.
