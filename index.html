Chapter 5 — Glimpses of India, Part II

"Coorg" is the second of three travel sketches that make up Glimpses of India, alongside "A Baker from Goa" and "Tea from Assam." Written by Lokesh Abrol, it reads less like a textbook lesson and more like a traveller's notebook — carrying the reader through the district's misty hills, its coffee-scented air, its rivers, and the distinct customs of the people who call it home.

Across the chapter, the author paints Coorg as a place where geography, history and everyday life are impossible to separate.

02

Where earth meets the mist

Location and geography of Coorg

Coorg — known today by its older name, Kodagu — is India's smallest district, folded into the Western Ghats of Karnataka. Its rolling hills, thick forest cover and cool, damp air set it apart from the plains around it, giving the district a climate closer to a hill station than to tropical India.

The terrain rises and falls in ridges cloaked in coffee estates, rainforest and spice gardens, making Coorg feel remote and self-contained even though it sits well within reach of the rest of Karnataka.

03

A cradle of monsoons

Climate and evergreen rainforests

Coorg is often called the cradle of the monsoons, since it catches rain from both the south-west and the retreating north-east monsoon, leaving the district green and well-watered for much of the year. This steady rainfall sustains dense evergreen rainforest across its slopes.

Winter, from September to March, is considered the most pleasant season to visit — cool, clear and comfortable — while the rains bring the forest fully alive, swelling streams and waterfalls across the hills.

04

Dress that carries history

Traditional cultural attire and the kupya

Kodava men traditionally wear the kupya, a long, dark, robe-like coat cinched at the waist with a striking red-and-gold sash called the chele kathi. Tucked into that sash sits a curved dagger, the peechekathi — a mark of the community's martial tradition worn even on ceremonial occasions.

Women, too, have a distinctive way of draping the sari, with the pleats folded at the back rather than the front and the loose end pinned over the shoulder — a style found nowhere else in the country and still worn with pride at Kodava weddings and festivals.
